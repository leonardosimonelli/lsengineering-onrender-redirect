From target motion to a planar mechanism: a practical workflow

Explore planar mechanism synthesis from a drawn trajectory or CSV points, compare candidates, and evaluate whether a proposed linkage can actually be built.

Updated November 3, 2026 · LS Engineering software lab

A designer usually starts with a mechanism and studies the path it produces. Reverse mechanism exploration starts with a motion requirement and looks for feasible mechanisms. This is useful at the concept stage, but it is an engineering search problem—not a guarantee of an exact match.

1. Describe the movement before choosing the linkage

Define the point whose motion you want to control. Draw its desired planar path or prepare a CSV of points. Make sure all coordinates have consistent units and a clear origin. A path drawn from a photograph or sketch is only as meaningful as its scale and reference frame.

Also clarify what the path alone does not specify: speed, direction of travel, force, torque, allowable envelope, cycle time, orientation of the moving body, and whether the motion is repeated or a single stroke. Different mechanisms may follow similar paths but behave very differently under load.

2. State the design constraints

Choose which families of joints and links are allowed. A rotating hinge, a sliding joint and a telescoping link imply different equations and manufacturing requirements. Record the ground attachment locations you can accommodate, minimum clearances, allowed dimensions and any restrictions on motors or actuators.

Very tight constraints can make the desired path unreachable. Very loose constraints may lead to mechanisms that are difficult to manufacture. This is a trade-off between exploration and practical applicability.

3. Generate and compare candidates

When a synthesis tool proposes a planar linkage, inspect the resulting layout and the path traced by its designated point. Compare the target path with the generated one using a consistent scale. A visually similar overlay may conceal significant local deviations, so inspect the entire stroke, including start/end positions and any sharp changes in curvature.

If the target path is open, check whether the mechanism can move smoothly throughout the intended travel and return safely. If it is closed, consider whether there is a continuous assembly configuration for the full cycle. A good numerical fit does not necessarily imply a usable device.

4. Validate mechanical feasibility

  • Check link lengths, assembly modes and possible dead-center positions.
  • Assess collision risks and the space required for all moving parts.
  • Estimate forces, joint reactions, bearings and actuator needs.
  • Confirm manufacturing tolerances and sufficient stiffness.
  • Complete any applicable mechanical, electrical and safety design verification independently.

Initial candidate exploration is not a substitute for CAD motion studies, stress calculations, collision checks or compliance assessments. Reverse synthesis should narrow down a design search, not replace engineering judgment.
